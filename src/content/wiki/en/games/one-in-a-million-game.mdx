A One in a Million game, as a thing you can open in a browser today, did not show up under that exact title when this page was researched on 14 November 2026. The words are real. They point at an idiom, a 1967 television show, and a cluster of newer games that only borrow the phrase. This page says which is which, and where to go if you actually wanted a rare-answer daily game.

## Is there a One in a Million game you can play today?

In ordinary English, "one in a million" means something so uncommon it stands out from a huge crowd. That is a figure of speech, not a rulesheet.

Wikipedia's disambiguation page lists a 1967 American game show produced by Merv Griffin Enterprises, and it keeps that show separate from a 1980 sitcom starring Shirley Hemphill that used the same title. The Classic TV Archive lists the game show as an ABC daytime series hosted by Danny O'Neil. Its premise line is that contestants must detect fact from fiction. The archive's episode guide runs to a final week ending 16 June 1967 and counts 55 episodes. A fan wiki spells the host's surname O'Neill and describes a panel of guests with unusual stories. Where those secondary writeups disagree on spelling or on the prize structure, this page stays with the archive's premise and dates, and with Wikipedia's production credit. It is not a game you can play on a daily website.

Searches for a modern "one in a million game" also collide with Krillion. The official Krillion FAQ calls the top of a prompt "the Krillion" or "the gem," and it says that gem is chosen for fun as well as rarity. Players and unofficial sites often say "one in a krillion," a pun on this idiom. That pun is not a second game living at krillion.io. The [Krillion](/games/krillion/) page explains the official labels, and it explains Noillirk, which is a different product whose name is Krillion spelled backwards.

Some apps and fan sites use "One in a Million" as a score nickname on their own boards. Those nicknames are not a published game called One in a Million, and they are not repeated here, because the boards disagree with each other and with Krillion's FAQ.

## What should you play if you wanted the rare answer?

[Rarest Answer](/) is the game on this site. The daily round deals up to seven prompts, the same set for everyone on your local date, resetting at local midnight. You name something that fits. A less-viewed accepted answer scores more. The tiers are Common (5), Familiar (10), Uncommon (20), Rare (40), Epic (70), and Legendary (100). Legendary is the least-viewed bucket on that prompt. It is not a claim that the answer happens once in a million tries, and it is not a count of what players typed.

There is a free [unlimited mode](/unlimited/) when you want more than the daily seven. Category rounds cover geography, animals, food, sports, screen, music, science, and history. One Spotlight, a 2× boost, is available once in a round. Afterward, the [rarest answers](/rarest-answers/) pages show what was accepted, so you can see the names that outscored the obvious one.

[Cosmillion](/games/cosmillion/) is the current daily game whose own top rank uses this kind of name. On its how-to-play page the top rank is One in a Galaxy, worth 100 points. Seven prompts, 25 seconds each, free with no account. Its bottom rank is Tourist, the answer everyone packed.

[Seldom](/games/seldom-game/) wants the same kind of answer and prints a low number for it. Five guesses, one question, and a zero is called a Seldom. [Jinxed](/games/jinxed-game/) pays up to 8,000 for a Primordial answer and then splits the points if a friend matched you. [Wonderhop](/games/wonderhop/) has a mode called Abyss for the answer almost nobody gives, next to modes that want the opposite.

## How do you chase a rare answer without a fake game?

Skip any site that offers today's "one in a million" solution as a list of codes or a guaranteed 100-point word. The games above score a valid member of a category. They do not have one secret string.

Start from a real member of the set. Then move one step away from the household example. A fruit prompt is not won by inventing a fruit. It is won by knowing a second fruit. The [how to find rare answers](/guides/how-to-find-rare-answers/) guide stays on that distinction. When you want to see the shape of a finished list, open [rarest answers](/rarest-answers/) rather than a page that claims to have decoded a show from 1967 into a browser streak.

[Games like Krillion](/games/games-like-krillion/) is the comparison if you are still sorting the daily games. [Games like Wordle](/games/games-like-wordle/) is the other habit: one hidden five-letter word, six tries, no rarity ladder. Wordle is a real, current game. One in a Million, as a 2026 daily puzzle, is not one I could verify.
