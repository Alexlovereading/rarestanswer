A classroom can use this game without a roster, a join code, or a lockstep buzzer. That is also the limit. There is no teacher dashboard, no per-student report, and no switch that makes the site a children's product. The useful part is the round itself: one shared daily, a timer you can turn off, and a reveal that shows why the obvious answer was cheap.

Play starts on the [homepage](/). The control list is the [rules page](/guides/how-to-play-rarest-answer/). This page is how a teacher might sequence those controls. It does not cite a learning study, because none was run for this guide.

## What does the privacy policy allow you to assume?

The [privacy policy](/privacy-policy/) says you do not create an account, and the site does not ask for a name, email, or password to play or to read the guides. Streak and round stats stay in localStorage on the device. Clearing site data clears them. A different browser does not have them. Answer checking runs in the browser against published prompt data.

The same policy says the game is not directed at children under 13, and that the site does not knowingly collect personal information from children. Krillion's [privacy policy](https://krillion.io/privacy) uses a similar line: it is not directed at children under 13, and a parent who believes a child has sent personal information can write to them. Assigning either game to a class of young children cuts against what those pages say. A secondary class, a club, or a teacher demo is the use this guide describes. It is not a certification, a school-district approval, or a claim that no third-party script can ever load. Ads and analytics on this site stay off unless their environment values are set, and they are not inside the play card.

A shared classroom computer is the practical catch. Local storage is per browser profile, not per student. The second student inherits the first student's streak, half-finished daily, and timer preference unless you use separate profiles or clear site data between turns. Say that out loud before you pass the laptop. It is not a bug in the lesson. It is how the storage works.

## How do you run one period on the shared daily?

Have every student open the daily on their own device, on the same local date. They receive the same prompts, up to seven, and fewer if the catalog is still short. That is what makes the scores comparable. Unlimited mode deals a different set and must not be used as the "official" class round if you wanted everyone on today's list.

Turn the timer off for a first look, if the device is the one teaching the rule. The clock is 25 seconds when it is on. A wrong guess costs one second and can be replaced until time runs out. The same guess again does not cost another second. With the timer off, a wrong guess only shakes, and Pass ends the prompt at zero. The share text will include the words "No timer," so a no-timer score is labeled.

After each prompt, leave the reveal up. It shows the tier and how many answers existed. A short list shows every answer, rarest first. A longer list shows ten of the rarest and ten of the most common. Ask the class which column their first instinct landed in. That conversation is the lesson. Collecting the rarest column into a notes file and replaying it tomorrow is not, because tomorrow's prompt is a different set. The [animal guide](/guides/rarest-answers-animals/) is a worked example you can project: Human and Bonobo are Common, and the Tapanuli orangutan is Legendary. That file's source is Wikidata plus Wikipedia pageviews (2026-10).

Spotlight doubles one scored prompt. In a demo, spend it on a specific answer once, and also say what doubling the common answer would have paid, so the waste is visible. The [scoring page](/guides/how-scoring-works-in-rarest-answer-games/) has the table: 5, 10, 20, 40, 70, 100, and a single double.

## What should you do when a category is empty?

Open the category only if you are willing to read the empty state. A category page says no prompts are loaded yet when that list is empty, and it points players back at the daily, which plays whatever is in the list. Do not ask the class to shout answers at a disabled Play button. [Geography trivia](/trivia/geography/) and [animal trivia](/trivia/animals/) both have published prompts. [Unlimited mode](/unlimited/) is the right room for a second round after the daily, or for a volunteer who already finished.

If you need a pile of map questions today, that is a different site. [Seterra](https://www.seterra.com/) says "Master Geography with +400 Map Quizzes" and offers educator printouts. [World Geography Games](https://www.world-geography-games.com/) calls its quizzes free teaching resources for class, including primary and secondary. Those are their claims about their quizzes. A correct pin there is not a tier here. The [geography tips](/guides/rarest-answer-game-tips-geography/) page keeps that difference.

## How is this different from a hosted class quiz?

[Kahoot](https://kahoot.com/), checked the same day, headlines "Make learning awesome!" and says it helps educators create lessons, helps students study, and turns a social occasion into a game. The page also offered a free start and listed paid plans, including prices and a limited offer ending 27 November 2026. This guide does not describe what the free tier includes, because the homepage did not spell out a feature matrix in the text we could quote. The shape is still different: a host writes or picks a quiz, and the room joins it.

[FunTrivia](https://www.funtrivia.com/) has an "Educational Quizzes" section labeled for parents, teachers, and students, plus a large library you can open without hosting. Its own pages disagreed about quiz counts that day, so no count is repeated here. [Unlimited trivia](/guides/best-unlimited-trivia-games/) is the longer list of sites that are not one shared daily.

None of those hosted or library quizzes pay more for the rarer correct answer. If that payment is the idea you wanted to teach, stay on this site for the round, and use the other sites as optional name practice.

## What should the class write down afterward?

The share grid. It is colored squares, a score, and maybe the lines "Spotlight used" and "No timer." It does not list the answers. Students can paste that grid into a class thread without spoiling the names for someone who has not played. Comparing grids compares buckets. It does not require you to collect emails.

Skip any house competition that needs a live lobby. This site does not have one. Krillion's Puffer Party does, and it is their game, with a random shared fuse. The [party ideas](/guides/rarest-answer-party-ideas/) page is for a group of friends using the share grid, not for a gradebook.
