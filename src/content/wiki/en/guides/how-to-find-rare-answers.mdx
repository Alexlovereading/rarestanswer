The prompt is easy on purpose. "Name a species of great ape" already has a famous reply, and that reply is worth the least. You want a correct name from the uncrowded end of the same list. The point table, the 25-second clock, and Spotlight are on [how to play](/guides/how-to-play-rarest-answer/). The names below are the published great-ape prompt.

## Why is the first name usually worth the least?

Memory hands you the member you have seen most often. In a room, those memories overlap, and the overlap is the low tier. On a narrow prompt, the high tier is the least famous legal member, not a word nobody has heard. On a wide prompt, such as naming a city, the same habit applies: leave the place you would put on a postcard.

On "Name a species of great ape," most people say human, chimpanzee, or gorilla. They are right about the category. The published list has eight names, rarest first: Tapanuli orangutan, Sumatran orangutan, Eastern gorilla, Western gorilla, Bornean orangutan, Chimpanzee, Bonobo, and Human. Human and Bonobo are Common, 5 points. Chimpanzee is Familiar, 10. "Gorilla" and "orangutan" are not rows. The file splits gorillas into western and eastern species, and orangutans into three species.

A common answer still beats a blank, because a rejected guess scores nothing. Type it when you cannot defend a more specific name, or when the clock is about to die. It is a weak result only when you had time to be more precise.

## How do you get more specific without leaving the prompt?

Take the answer you were about to type and add the qualifier a textbook would add. A longer joke is not the move.

- "Gorilla" becomes a species. The list accepts Western gorilla (Uncommon, 20) and Eastern gorilla (Rare, 40).
- "Orangutan" becomes one of three species. The file does not have a plain orangutan row.
- "Chimp" does not sneak onto a higher tier. The chimpanzee row's aliases include "chimp" and "common chimpanzee," so those spellings are Familiar, 10 points. Bonobo is its own row, and it is Common, the same 5 points as Human. "Pygmy chimpanzee" is stored on Bonobo, not above it.

The move travels. A cheese prompt wants a place and a milk. A cast prompt wants someone who is not the face on the poster. [Stranger Things characters](/guides/stranger-things-characters-trivia/) is that drill, and the stored tiers are on the [answer page](/answers/stranger-things-characters/).

Hold the new name to one sentence: "This is a kind of X, because..." If the sentence needs a wink, do not submit it. If it is plain and true, it is the right shape.

## How do a place and a later split change the rank?

People remember the member from the classroom map. They forget the neighboring member that is just as legal.

In this file, Bornean orangutan is Uncommon (20), Eastern gorilla is Rare (40), Sumatran orangutan is Epic (70), and Tapanuli orangutan is Legendary (100). Those ranks are English Wikipedia pageviews summed over the 12 complete months from November 2025 through October 2026, compared only with the other names on this prompt. The source line is Wikidata plus Wikipedia pageviews (2026-10). That label is the end of the window, not the view count for October alone. The sums are on [great ape species](/answers/great-ape-species/). Every other prompt is on [rarest answers](/rarest-answers/).

The biology is a separate fact, and it explains why the top name is a fair specific answer rather than a trick. Wikipedia describes the Tapanuli orangutan, *Pongo tapanuliensis*, as a species restricted to South Tapanuli on Sumatra, one of three living orangutan species, and described as its own species in 2017. The same article calls it the rarest great ape. Source: [Tapanuli orangutan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tapanuli_orangutan). A name tied to one district, and only split off in 2017, is not what a general audience says first. The game does not score how many animals are left. It scores the stored tier.

Use history the same way you use a map. Ask what was split off, renamed, or described later. On a film prompt, a side character can be legal and less typed than the lead. Step outside the famous example without stepping outside the prompt.

Aliases are part of the check. Eastern gorilla's stored alias is "Gorilla beringei." "Grauer's gorilla" and "eastern lowland gorilla" are not stored, so they do not land on that Rare row. Another way of writing Human does not make Human rare. The matcher, described on the rules page, accepts capitalization, accents, punctuation, a leading "the," and one wrong character on a longer name when only one answer fits. It does not invent an alias.

Tiers are buckets. Western gorilla and Bornean orangutan are both Uncommon here even though their view totals differ. The next name down is not automatically a full tier cheaper.

## When is a higher tier worth the risk?

The gaps are not equal.

- Common to Familiar is 5 points.
- Familiar to Uncommon is 10.
- Uncommon to Rare is 20.
- Rare to Epic, and Epic to Legendary, are 30 each.

The expensive mistake is leaving a Rare name you can spell to chase a Legendary name you cannot. Rare is already 40. If the "rarer" idea is a coin flip between Epic and a rejection, the Rare name you were holding can be the better score. Embarrassment is not in the scoring. Five points is. On a huge set, such as cities or foods, you do not need the single least-viewed member. You need a true member from outside the handful everyone says. Chasing a mythical top name is how the box stays empty.

Keep two answers, not one. The first is the ordinary legal name, ready if the clock dies. The second is the more specific name, and you submit it only while you can still explain it. Players lose the prompt when they fall in love with the second idea and either misspell it or leave the category.

Before you commit, ask three questions. Does the prompt's noun still fit? Could someone who only read the question defend the name? Are you spelling the distinctive word? The points on this example sit on "Tapanuli," not on a creative spelling of "orangutan." If you fail those questions, submit the best specific name you can defend.

## When should you use Spotlight?

You get one double for the round: 10, 20, 40, 80, 140, or 200 as you climb from Common to Legendary. It does nothing for a rejected answer, and it does not change the tier. Doubling a name you were going to type anyway turns 5 into 10.

Spend it when three things are true.

1. You have a specific word, not a mood.
2. The one-sentence test passes.
3. You are not saving it for a later prompt you actually know better. The prompts are not ordered from easy to hard. If the animal prompt is the one you understand, the boost belongs there even when it comes up first.

If every remaining idea is ordinary, double the best specific guess you can spell, not the one you are least sure of. Timer off or timer on, you still get one boost. A doubled Common answer is 10, which loses to an undoubled Rare answer at 40. That comparison is in the [point table](/guides/how-to-play-rarest-answer/).

## How should practice differ from the daily?

The [daily round](/) is the streak. Play it as a match: two names, the sentence test, Spotlight on the prompt you understand, and the ordinary name only as a fallback. Do not use the daily to find out whether a wild guess is on the list. The reveal teaches that after the prompt is spent.

[Unlimited mode](/unlimited/) is the practice room. Turn the timer off and force the second name. Then turn the timer on and see whether that name still arrives before you retreat to the first. One category at a time is faster to learn. [Animal trivia](/trivia/animals/) stays on animals. Mixing every category feels like a real round and teaches less per minute.

Play the daily first if you care about today's streak, and practice after. The daily set is the same for everyone that day. It is not the same set as a random unlimited prompt. A name you just saw on a reveal is not an answer key for a different question.

## What should you read on the reveal?

A short list is shown in full, rarest first. A longer list shows the ten rarest and the ten most common. Read them in this order.

Most common first. Keep the pattern, not the proper noun. If the common column is full of capitals, the next geography prompt is where you refuse capitals. If it is the leads of a show, the next screen prompt is where you refuse the poster. The names expire with the prompt. The pattern does not.

The rare column second. Notice the shape: a region, a later split, a side character with a job. Copy the shape next time. Memorizing today's top city will not name tomorrow's animal.

The size of the list third. A Legendary hit on eight answers is knowledge of a small set. A Legendary hit on a huge set means the selection worked. Only the second one tells you the habit is holding on the prompts that fill most rounds.

Then drop three habits that feel clever and score like the ordinary answer, or score nothing.

- A longer spelling of the common row. If "common chimpanzee" is an alias of chimpanzee, the extra word bought Familiar, not Epic.
- Leaving the set to sound obscure. A film about the subject is not the subject. Some puzzles in the [daily trivia roundup](/guides/best-daily-trivia-games/) want a different skill. This one wants a legal member from the uncrowded end.
- Spending the clock on a spelling you cannot finish. A Rare name you can submit beats an Epic name that never leaves the box.
