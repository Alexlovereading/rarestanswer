Finding rare answers is the skill behind a rarest-answer round. The prompt is easy on purpose. "Name a species of great ape" already has a famous reply in your mouth, and that reply is worth the least. You are looking for a legal name other people will not type.

This page is the technique. The [rules, tiers, and Spotlight boost](/guides/how-to-play-rarest-answer/) are separate, and the [in-round decisions](/guides/rarest-answer-strategy/) come after you can produce a second candidate. The names below are the sample prompt that ships with this site.

## What does finding rare answers actually mean?

A prompt asks for one member of a set. Every accepted member is correct. Rarity is a rank inside that set, not a prize for sounding strange. You are trying to be the last honest answer in line.

Memory will not hand you a random legal member. It hands you the one you have seen most often. In a room, those memories overlap, and the overlap is the common tier. On a narrow prompt, "rare" means the least famous legal member, not a word nobody has heard. On a wide prompt such as "name a city," the same habit applies: leave the capital you would put on a postcard.

## Why is the first name you think of almost always common?

On "Name a species of great ape," most people say human, chimpanzee, or gorilla. They are right about the category and wrong about the score.

The sample list has eight accepted names, rarest first. Human is tier 1, Common, 5 points. Chimpanzee is tier 2, Familiar, 10 points. Western gorilla is Familiar too. The plain word "gorilla" is not its own row. The file splits gorillas into western and eastern species, and orangutans into three species. An unspecialized headline stops where the crowd stops.

A common answer still beats a blank, because a rejected guess scores nothing. It is a bad result only when you had time to be more specific. [Strategy](/guides/rarest-answer-strategy/) is the choice between those two.

## How do you go long-tail without leaving the prompt?

Long-tail here means a more specific true name, not a longer joke. Take the answer you were about to type and add the qualifier a textbook would add.

- "Gorilla" becomes a species. The sample accepts Western gorilla (Familiar, 10) and Eastern gorilla (Epic, 70).
- "Orangutan" becomes an island, or part of one. The file has three rows.
- "Chimp" becomes a species question. Bonobo is its own row, Uncommon, 20 points. The chimpanzee row's aliases include "chimp" and "common chimpanzee," so the short name does not sneak onto a rarer tier.

The move travels. "Name a cheese" wants a region and a milk. "Name a Stranger Things character" wants someone who is not Eleven, which is why that cast has its own page of [overlooked characters](/guides/stranger-things-characters-trivia/).

## How do regional and historical cuts change the answer?

People remember the place they have seen on a classroom map. They forget the neighboring place that is just as legal.

In the sample, Bornean orangutan is Uncommon (20), Sumatran orangutan is Rare (40), and Tapanuli orangutan is Legendary (100). Those are ranks inside this file, not a live count of what players typed today.

The biology explains why the top name is a fair long-tail rather than a trick. Wikipedia describes the Tapanuli orangutan, *Pongo tapanuliensis*, as a species restricted to South Tapanuli on Sumatra, one of three living orangutan species alongside the Sumatran and Bornean orangutans, and described as a distinct species in 2017. The same article calls it the rarest great ape. Source: [Tapanuli orangutan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tapanuli_orangutan). A name tied to one district, and only split off as its own species in 2017, is not what a general audience blurts out. Biological rarity and guess-rarity line up here. They are not the same measurement. The game scores the name among accepted answers. It does not score how many animals are left.

Use history the same way you use a map. Ask what was split off, renamed, or described later. On a film prompt, a side character is often legal and less typed than the reboot lead. The famous example swallows the category. Your job is to step outside it without stepping outside the truth.

## When does a sub-category beat the headline?

The sample question says "species." The eight rows are Tapanuli orangutan, Eastern gorilla, Sumatran orangutan, Bonobo, Bornean orangutan, Western gorilla, Chimpanzee, and Human. "Orangutan" and "gorilla" are not rows.

Generate the rows on purpose. Name the headline, list the real branches you can picture (islands, ranges, sister species), and pick the branch you learned last. Then say, in one sentence, why it still fits the prompt.

Aliases are part of the check. In the sample, "Grauer's gorilla" and "eastern lowland gorilla" are aliases of Eastern gorilla, not extra species. Typing the alias should land on that Epic row. It should not invent a gorilla above the list. A synonym of Human does not make Human rare.

Tiers are buckets. Bonobo and Bornean orangutan are both Uncommon here even though their rarity numbers differ. The next name down is not automatically a full tier cheaper. Points live on the tier: Common 5, Familiar 10, Uncommon 20, Rare 40, Epic 70, Legendary 100. The full card is the [how to play](/guides/how-to-play-rarest-answer/) page.

One limit on this sample. Its source field is fixture, and the popularity figures are placeholders until the data build replaces them. Use the names and the tier order. Do not quote those figures as Wikipedia traffic. A scored page for the prompt belongs with the other lists on [rarest answers](/rarest-answers/) and at [great ape species](/answers/great-ape-species/).

## How do you keep a rare guess valid?

A rare answer the list rejects is worth less than "chimpanzee." Before you commit, ask three questions.

Does the prompt's noun still fit? A subspecies nickname might be an alias, as Grauer's gorilla is, or it might be a rank the list does not accept. Could a non-expert defend the name from the wording alone? "Tapanuli orangutan" is an orangutan species, and orangutans are great apes. A zoo, a film, or a village is not. Are you spelling the distinctive word? The points sit on "Tapanuli," not on a creative spelling of "orangutan." This game forgives capitalization, accents, and a small typo on a longer name. It does not forgive a different animal.

If you fail those questions, submit the best specific name you can defend. The [daily games roundup](/guides/best-daily-trivia-games/) is full of puzzles that want a different skill. This one wants a legal member from the uncrowded end of the set.

## What should the last few seconds look like?

Keep two answers, not one. The first is the common legal name, ready if the clock dies. The second is the long-tail name, and you submit it only if you can still explain it. Players lose the prompt when they fall in love with the second idea and either misspell it or leave the category.

Then read the reveal, not just the total. The useful part is which names were common and which were available. Tomorrow's set will be different. Your habit will not, unless you look. The daily round starts on the [homepage](/), and unlimited play is there when you want reps more than a streak.
